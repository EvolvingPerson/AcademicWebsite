Safety steps

10 steps for vetting a dating app match

Tap any step to open it. Steps 1 through 8 check the person you are talking to. Steps 9 and 10 protect you whoever they turn out to be. In my research, the most distressing harms on dating apps came from real people, not fake profiles, so the last two steps matter even when everything else checks out.

Stop now

Any one of these is enough.

  • They ask for money, gift cards, or cryptocurrency
  • They ask for intimate images before you have met
  • They keep refusing a live video call
  • They threaten you, or keep pushing after you say no

Slow down

Two or three together means stop.

  • They want to leave the app right away
  • They talk about love or fate within days
  • Every photo is a polished solo portrait
  • Their answers stay vague when you ask for specifics
  • You can find no trace of them anywhere else
  • They are online at hours that do not fit where they live
1

Start with your gut
  • If something feels off, that feeling is information. You do not need proof to unmatch.
  • Notice when you are explaining away something you would question if a friend described it.

Do this: Decide now that “this feels off” is reason enough to stop talking, unmatch, or leave.

2

Look closely at the photos
  • Many fake profiles now use faces made with artificial intelligence (AI). Those faces never existed before, so a reverse image search will not find them.
  • Real people have candid photos: friends in the frame, ordinary backgrounds, mixed lighting, different haircuts and ages. Be wary when every photo is a polished solo portrait.
  • AI faces often slip at the edges: mismatched earrings, warped glasses frames, strange hands or teeth, garbled text in the background.

Do this: Screenshot a photo and search it with Google Lens anyway. It still catches stolen photos, which are common. If the photo turns up under another name, stop.

3

Ask for specifics
  • Scammers write with AI now, so polished grammar proves nothing. Imperfect English proves nothing either; many real people write in a second language.
  • What scripts and chatbots handle badly is follow-up. Ask about something specific they told you earlier: the name of the place they work, or what happened at the event they mentioned.
  • Watch for answers that are warm but vague, or that change the subject.

Do this: Ask two concrete follow-up questions. Real people answer with details; scripts answer with feelings.

4

Check their footprint outside the app
  • Look them up by name, workplace, or linked account. A real person usually leaves some trace: a LinkedIn page, a tagged photo, a friend in common.
  • Check that the details match across places: age, job, city, and story.
  • A verified badge usually means their face matched a selfie, not that the app checked who they are. Only 9 of the 24 apps my team studied document photo or ID verification at all.

Do this: Search their name with their city or workplace before you meet. No trace at all is a reason to slow down, not proof, since some people keep a low profile on purpose.

5

Get on a live video call
  • A short, unscripted video call is still one of the best checks. Repeated excuses, like a broken camera or a bad signal, are a reason to stop.
  • Live face swapping tools now exist, so make the call interactive. Ask them to turn their head side to side or pass a hand in front of their face. Swapped faces often blur or glitch when something crosses them.
  • If your app has its own video chat, use it, so you do not have to share your phone number yet.

Do this: Ask for a five minute video call before you agree to meet.

6

Watch the pace
  • Big declarations of love or fate within days, before you have met, are a classic pressure tactic.
  • Be careful when someone pushes to move to WhatsApp, Telegram, or text right away. Once you leave the app, the app cannot see the conversation or act on your report.
  • Notice if they disappear and reappear, or are online at hours that do not fit where they say they live.

Do this: Keep the conversation on the app until you have met in person. If they will not, that tells you something.

7

Never send money
  • Any request for money, gift cards, cryptocurrency, or help with an “investment” is a reason to stop, however good the story.
  • Guard information that can be turned into money, too: your full name with your birthday, your workplace, your home address, and any account details.

Do this: If money comes up, stop. Report the profile to the app, and report the scam to the Federal Trade Commission at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

8

Hold on to intimate images
  • Sextortion moves fast: a flirty match, a quick move to another app, a request to trade photos, then a threat to share yours unless you pay. It can all happen within hours.
  • Do not send intimate images to someone you have not met in person.
  • If it is already happening: do not pay, do not delete the conversation, take screenshots, block the person, and report them to the app and to the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) at tips.fbi.gov.

Do this: If you are 18 or older and the images were taken when you were 18 or older, use StopNCII.org to stop them from spreading on participating platforms. It works from a digital fingerprint, so your images are never uploaded. For images taken before age 18, use Take It Down from the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children. Under federal law, platforms must now remove intimate images shared without consent within 48 hours of your request.

9

Check what your own profile gives away
  • Run a reverse image search on your own photos. If one leads to your Instagram or LinkedIn, a match can find your full name, workplace, and neighborhood in minutes.
  • Crop or skip photos that show your building, street signs, a school or work logo, a uniform, or your license plate.
  • If your app shows how far away you are, turn off exact distance when you can. Exact distance, checked from a few spots, can pin down where you live.

Do this: Use different photos on the app than on your public social media, and keep your last name, workplace, and neighborhood off your profile.

10

Plan the first meeting
  • Only 8 of the 24 apps my team studied give any guidance for meeting in person, so make your own plan.
  • Meet somewhere public and busy, and get there and home on your own.
  • Send a friend their name, a screenshot of their profile, where you are going, and a time you will check in. Agree on what your friend should do if you do not.
  • Choose a place where you are comfortable being seen. For LGBTQ+ people, a first date can carry the risk of being outed.

Do this: Text a friend your plan before you leave, and check in when you get home.

How 24 dating apps compare

Which apps document the most safety?

My team read the public safety pages, terms of service, and help pages of 24 North American dating platforms on April 2, 2025, and again on April 2, 2026. We checked whether each platform documents specific safety tools, rules against harassment and discrimination, privacy practices, and what it charges for, then gave each platform a score out of 12.

A higher score means the app says it does more. It does not prove the app is safer to use, and no score replaces the ten safety steps.

0 of 24

Say what happens after you report

No platform tells you whether the person you reported was warned, suspended, or is still on the app.

17 of 24

Charge for a safety feature

71 percent lock at least one safety-related feature, like messaging or hiding your profile, behind a paywall.

1 → 9

Added photo verification

The biggest improvement in a year. Safety tip pages also grew, from 11 platforms to 21.

0

Updated anti-discrimination rules

Not one platform changed its discrimination policies between 2025 and 2026.

How the score works

1

Pick the platforms

We started with the 120 most popular dating apps in North American app stores. We kept 24 that were still operating, published enough about their safety practices to code, and were not near copies of another app from the same company. Seven belong to Match Group, four to Spark Networks, and 13 are independent.

2

Read what each app publishes

Terms of service, community guidelines, and safety centers or safety tips pages. In 2026 we also checked app store listings, help pages, and press releases. We did not test features inside the apps, so the study shows what platforms say, not what users experience.

3

Code each feature

A feature counts as 1 if the app clearly describes it, 0.5 if it is only partly or indirectly described, and 0 if we could find nothing. AI tools pulled the exact policy text, and a research assistant checked every platform against the original pages. Two coders independently re-coded six platforms and agreed on 101 of 102 decisions.

4

Add it up

Each platform’s score adds up 12 safety dimensions, all weighted equally, for a maximum of 12. We call 9 or more high, 5 to 8.9 moderate, and below 5 low. A high score means broad documentation, not proof that a feature works.

Why it matters

The quiet harms are the ones that hurt most

In my survey of 867 U.S. adults who had used a dating app in the past 60 days, 89 percent had experienced at least one harm in that window. The most common harms, like unwanted sexual messages, were the least distressing. The rarer ones, like pressure after saying no and someone finding you offline, were the most distressing, and the in-app block button reaches them least.

Vetting a profile is a first step. If something does happen, tell someone you trust, save the messages, and make a plan.
