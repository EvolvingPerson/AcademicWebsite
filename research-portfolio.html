Dating App Research Portfolio

Five manuscripts examining digital harm, queer space, platform safety, and measurement science in dating applications.

01

Digital Hetrification and Double Displacement

Vincent A. Jones II, Ed.D.; Laurie Essig, Ph.D.

When straight users flood queer dating spaces like Grindr, they bring mainstream norms with them and erode what made those spaces safe in the first place. We call this digital hetrification.

 02

Safer Than Before?

Vincent Jones II; Celeta Vanderpool; Erela Datuowei; Apeksha Mewani, Ed.D.; Erin Toussaint Jacques, Ph.D., Ed.D.

We tracked safety features across 24 dating platforms over a year. Not one protects users after they report abuse, and most charge extra for basic safety tools.

 03

Risk Factors, Protective Action, and the Frequency to Distress Dissociation

Vincent Jones II; Erin Toussaint Jacques, Ph.D., Ed.D.; Apeksha Mewani, Ed.D.

The harms that happen most often on dating apps are not the ones that hurt people the most. We call this the frequency to distress dissociation, and it means platforms are measuring the wrong things.

 04

Development and Validation of the DIHS-6D

Vincent Jones II; Erin Toussaint Jacques, Ph.D., Ed.D.; Apeksha Mewani, Ed.D.

The first validated tool designed to measure what actually happens to people on dating apps, covering six domains of harm from harassment to stalking. No such instrument existed before.

Development and Validation of the DAHRSES

Vincent Jones II; Erin Toussaint Jacques, Ph.D., Ed.D.; Apeksha Mewani, Ed.D.

A tool that measures whether people believe they can handle harmful situations on dating apps. Those who do are more likely to take protective action, which means these skills can be taught.

Paper 01 · Queer digital space

Digital Hetrification and Double Displacement

Full title: Digital Hetrification and Double Displacement: Straight Users and the Erosion of Queer Space on Grindr

Authors: Vincent A. Jones II, Ed.D. (York College, The City University of New York); Laurie Essig, Ph.D. (Middlebury College)

Central Argument

Hetrification, the process by which heterosexual people cumulatively displace queer culture from queer spaces, extends from physical venues into digital queer counterpublics like Grindr. Straight users bring mainstream norms with them, eroding the safety and cultural specificity that made these spaces valuable to lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, and queer communities.

Key empirical finding: In a general population sample of 867 dating app users, 30.9% of Grindr users identified as straight, far exceeding the 1% to 7% reported in prior studies focused on men who have sex with men.

Original Contribution

The concept of double displacement: marginalized users face secondary marginalization within the queer counterpublic designed to shelter them, compounded by economic gatekeeping of safety features.

Three Mechanisms Proposed

Assumed Shared Identity Degradation

Straight users undermine the implicit trust and shared identity that define queer digital spaces.

Erosion of Community Norms

Community specific interaction norms are diluted as mainstream heteronormative expectations infiltrate the platform.

Platform Complicity

Structural enablement by the platform itself: design choices that allow and even encourage cross-community use without safeguards.

Paper 02 · Platform safety policy

Safer Than Before?

Full title: Safer Than Before? A Content Analysis of Safety Features and Policy Frameworks Across 24 Dating Platforms, 2025 to 2026

Authors: Vincent Jones II; Celeta Vanderpool; Erela Datuowei; Apeksha Mewani, Ed.D.; Erin Toussaint Jacques, Ph.D., Ed.D.

Design: Longitudinal content analysis of 24 North American dating platforms at two time points (April 2025 and April 2026) across 32 policy dimensions.

24

Platforms Analyzed

North American dating platforms assessed across 32 policy dimensions

0%

Post-Report Protection

Absent across all 24 platforms. No platform protects users after they report abuse.

71%

Paywalled Safety

17 of 24 platforms paywalled at least one safety relevant feature

Key Findings

  • Photo and identification verification increased from 1 to 9 platforms (p = .008)
  • Post-report user protection was absent across all 24 platforms (0%)
  • 17 platforms (71%) paywalled at least one safety relevant feature
  • Discrimination protections were entirely unchanged across the study period
  • Composite safety scores ranged from 1.5 to 10 out of 12

Translation to practice: Health educators should build programming on risk recognition, boundary setting, and critical evaluation of platform safety claims, because platforms cannot be relied upon to protect users.

Paper 03 · Risk and protective action

Risk Factors, Protective Action, and the Frequency to Distress Dissociation

Full title: Risk Factors, Protective Action, and the Frequency to Distress Dissociation in Dating App Interpersonal Harm

Authors: Vincent Jones II; Erin Toussaint Jacques, Ph.D., Ed.D.; Apeksha Mewani, Ed.D.

Sample: 867 U.S. adults completing the Dating-App Interpersonal Harm Scale (DIHS-6D)

The most common harms (unsolicited sexual content) were the least distressing; the rarest (stalking, coercion) were the most distressing. Item level rank gaps reached 29 positions.

Bars are illustrative of rank order, not exact values. This dissociation shows that platform metrics based on report volume fundamentally misrepresent the psychological burden of dating app harm.

Other Key Findings

  • Platform Ecosystem as Risk Factor

    Use of apps oriented to lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, and queer users predicted harm beyond sexual minority identity. The platform ecosystem, not identity, is the proximal risk factor, refining Minority Stress Theory.

  • Sensitization, Not Habituation

    Cumulative lifetime exposure predicted greater distress. Repeated harm sensitizes rather than habituates.

  • Coercion Suppresses Action

    Sexual coercion suppressed rather than mobilized protective action, a critical finding for intervention design.

Paper 05 · Self-efficacy

Development and Validation of the DAHRSES

Full title: Development and Initial Validation of the Dating-App Harm Response Self-Efficacy Scale (DAHRSES)

Authors: Vincent Jones II; Erin Toussaint Jacques, Ph.D., Ed.D.; Apeksha Mewani, Ed.D.

Adapted from the General Self-Efficacy Scale (Schwarzer and Jerusalem, 1995) and grounded in Bandura's social cognitive theory.

Psychometric Performance

Sample

Full sample N = 461; subsample n = 253 for discriminant and criterion validity analyses

Internal Consistency

Cronbach's alpha = .920, excellent reliability across the 10 item scale

Factor Structure

Well fitting unidimensional structure confirmed through confirmatory factor analysis

Convergent Validity

Correlation with the General Self-Efficacy Scale: r = .489 to .619, p < .001

Discriminant Validity

Modest correlation with social desirability (r = .203), lower than the General Self-Efficacy Scale benchmark

Criterion Validity

Positive association with protective behavior use (r = .185), strengthening after controlling for harm frequency (r = .229)

The Core Insight

Those who believe they can handle harmful situations on dating apps are more likely to take protective action, which means these skills can be taught.

The link between DAHRSES scores and actual protective behavior identifies harm response self-efficacy as a modifiable target for health education intervention. Unlike platform safety features that remain inaccessible or absent, self-efficacy is a skill that can be cultivated through structured programming.

Intervention implication: Health educators can design curricula that build confidence in responding to dating app harm, translating directly into safer user behavior regardless of platform safety infrastructure.
