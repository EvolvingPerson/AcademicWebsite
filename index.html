About

Scholar. Educator. Public Health Advocate.

Dr. Vincent Jones II is an Assistant Professor of Community Health and Director of the Health Promotion Center at York College, The City University of New York. His research sits at the intersection of e-health, digital environments, and public health equity. He studies what happens to people on dating apps: how often harm occurs, how much it hurts, who is affected most, and what helps people respond.

With colleagues at York and Lehman, he developed two validated instruments for this work: the Dating-App Interpersonal Harm Scale, which measures six kinds of harm and how much each one hurts, and the Dating-App Harm Response Self-Efficacy Scale, which measures confidence in responding. His current projects extend that work to platform safety policy, to the people least able to ask for help, and to harm from AI agents. Alongside the research, he writes for general audiences about dating culture, digital safety, and queer life. In May 2026 he was an invited panelist at the United Nations, at a side event of the 59th Session of the Commission on Population and Development, on how online dating is reshaping family formation.

Dr. Jones holds a Doctorate in Education and brings both scholarly depth and cultural insight to conversations about how technology shapes modern relationships, identity, and community.

Research & Publications

What Dating Apps Don't Measure: Harm, Protection, and Equity

Supported by the Professional Staff Congress of The City University of New York and the Mellon Foundation | 2025 to present

Dr. Jones leads a research program on what happens to people on dating apps, who gets hurt most, and what actually protects them. With colleagues at York College and Lehman College, he developed and validated two instruments: the Dating-App Interpersonal Harm Scale (DIHS-6D), which measures six kinds of harm and asks, for each one, both how often it happens and how much it hurts; and the Dating-App Harm Response Self-Efficacy Scale (DAHRSES), which measures a person's confidence that they can respond.

In a national survey of 867 adults who had used a dating app in the previous 60 days, 89 percent reported at least one harm. The most common harms were the least distressing, and the rarest were the most distressing. The harms that hurt most, pressure after saying no and being tracked or found offline, were the ones that in-app tools such as blocking and reporting reached least. Harm concentrated among women, people of color, sexual and gender minorities, and people who conceal their app use. Confidence in responding predicted protective action over and above how much harm a person had experienced.

A companion review of 24 North American dating platforms in 2025 and 2026 found that none document what happens after a user files a report, and that 71 percent place at least one safety feature behind a paywall. Four papers from this program are under review or submitted. The DIHS-6D validation paper is available as a preprint on SSRN.

Selected Talks

Invited panelist · United Nations

Impact of Online Dating on Family Formation: From Community-Mediated Introductions to Algorithm-Driven Encounters

Side event, 59th Session of the UN Commission on Population and Development | United Nations, New York | May 2026

Before an audience of diplomats, scholars, faith leaders, and youth advocates, Dr. Jones argued that dating app safety belongs to public policy, education, and platform culture rather than to personal responsibility alone. Drawing on his team's harm scale, their review of North American platforms, and the gap between how often harms occur and how much they hurt, he set out five priorities: transparency about what happens after a user reports harm, safety features that are not sold by subscription tier, dating app safety in health education curricula, policy designed with the people most affected, and accountability for the user cultures platforms cultivate. The panel was moderated by the Sikh Human Rights Group.

What Dating Apps Don't Measure: A Public Health Approach to Harm, Protection, and Equity

York College Faculty Symposium, The City University of New York | November 5, 2026

A 30-minute talk built around five questions: what actually happens on dating apps, why we didn't know, whether people can just block and report, who gets hurt the most, and what actually protects people. The session opens with a live audience poll and closes with a hands-on activity in which attendees take the place of a dating app user, read two conversations, name the harm, and choose a response.
